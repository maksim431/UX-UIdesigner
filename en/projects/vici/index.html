vici

VICI is a large food company and one of Europe’s leading producers of fish and seafood.

The brand is best known for its crab sticks made from surimi — its signature product. For decades the red packs with the VICI logo were the most popular product in this category across the post-Soviet countries.

in this case

  1. A website without a cart that sells.

    +25% clicks from recipes to products — the purchase happens at the shelf.

  2. People come here not for herring but for dinner.

    How one insight turned a catalog into a cooking magazine: +35% depth, +40% time.

  3. A beautiful catalog — the hypothesis that lost.

    Why redesigning the showcase didn’t solve the main problem — dead ends.

  4. A “cheap frozen food” brand got younger without changing the product.

    +12 pp of the 18–34 audience — through design, not the recipe.

  1. Task

Design a minimalist website based on the brand’s new design code after the rebrand and adapt it to all devices without losing its visual appeal.

The business goal: increase time on site and build deferred demand — the desire to buy the products in an offline store. The site has no cart, so it has to sell through shopper behavior.

  1. Research

I looked for where the business goals (offline sales growth) meet real shopper behavior.

Consumption patterns. People rarely look for the specs of herring or shrimp on a producer’s website. Their real need is to decide what to cook for dinner or find an idea for a festive table. Takeaway: sell not the raw product but the result — a delicious dish; the site should work as a lifestyle cooking magazine, not a warehouse showcase.

Site map. The “Products” and “Recipes” sections existed in isolation: a user would open the fish catalog, hit a dead end and leave. Takeaway: cyclic cross-linking is needed — the catalog suggests recipes, and a recipe leads to specific brand SKUs.

Context. People often check the site on their phone while standing at a supermarket shelf. Takeaway: the mobile version must be easy to use with one hand.

  1. Hypotheses

Hypothesis A — a beautiful catalog. Refresh the showcase in the new design code. The site becomes more modern, but the dead ends remain and time on site won’t grow.

Hypothesis B — a separate recipe blog. Content attracts traffic, but if it doesn’t lead to products, inspiration won’t turn into a purchase.

Hypothesis C — a cooking magazine with a “product ↔ recipe” loop. Build the user journey around recipes with seamless links between inspiration and a specific product. Then people spend more time on the site and leave with a ready purchase scenario.

  1. Which hypothesis I chose and why

I went with hypothesis C: it’s the only one that both increases time on site and turns it into a purchase at the shelf. Hypothesis A changes the shell but not behavior, and hypothesis B gives inspiration without a path to the product.

  1. Designing the mockups

  1. A two-way link between catalog and recipes. Every product page has a “What to cook with it” block with relevant recipes. A recipe page has an “Ingredients from VICI” block linking to the product page.

    Why it works: the user enters an “engagement loop” and leaves the site with a specific product in mind. The cross-linking also helps search rankings.

  1. Smart recommendations at the end of a recipe. After a recipe for shrimp croissants, the site suggests other quick breakfasts (a sticky block or infinite scroll on mobile).

    Why it works: there’s always a next step — the session doesn’t end and time on site grows.

  1. A tag system. “Quick”, “For breakfast”, “Seafood” — across recipes and products.

    Why it works: the right content is found instantly, without searching or reloads — fewer bounces.

  1. A mobile version for the shopper at the shelf. Larger tap targets, sticky navigation, and recipes laid out so the step-by-step instructions are easy to scroll with one hand without losing sight of the brand’s ingredients.

    Why it works: the site helps at the moment of choice in the store and becomes a “cookbook in your pocket” people come back to.

  1. Presentation and defense

I walked the client from shopper behavior to the decisions.

How a site without a cart sells. The key question is how to tell the site works for offline sales. The argument: we measure hidden conversion — clicks from recipes to product pages, page depth and time on site.

Recipes at the center, not the catalog. For a producer, this changes the site’s role from showcase to magazine. The argument: people come for a dinner idea, and that’s what brings them to the shelf.

  1. Handoff to development

The design project is built systematically: screens, states and modals are built with Auto Layout and linked to a UI kit with a design system, styles and components in all states. Animations are annotated. Along with the mockups, developers received a ready content-link schema — which recipes lead to which products.

  1. Results

Page depth +35%, time on site +40%: thanks to the “product ↔ recipe” loop, users stopped ending the session after one page.

Hidden conversion +25%: a quarter more clicks from recipes to product pages. People leave with a dinner plan and know which pack to look for on the shelf.

Mobile experience: −20% bounces, +15% return visits: the site became a “cookbook in your pocket”, raising user LTV.

18–34 audience +12 pp: a modern, airy UI and food aesthetics helped the brand shed its “cheap frozen food” image.

  1. Where and how I used AI

  • Data analysis. With an LLM I processed large volumes of customer reviews and frequent search queries and ran a quick audit of the old site architecture — a basis for hypotheses in hours.

  • Information architecture. AI analyzed the recipe base and catalog and generated a cross-link matrix with specific SKUs — the prototype with realistic content was ready sooner.

  • Content tagging. An LLM clustered recipes by scenario, time and ingredients — developers got a ready tag schema.

  • Responsive layouts. Basic mobile grids were generated by AI; the time saved went into hand-refining critical UX scenarios.

  • Figma routine. Structuring and renaming layers, checking for missing styles and generating the design system — with AI tools.
