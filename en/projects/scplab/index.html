sCPlab

SCP LAB is a mobile app that acts as a personal trainer for professional athletes. It analyzes the user’s physical data and generates an adaptive training plan to build strength. The core features are video instructions from professionals with individually tuned load, a progress tracker, smart reminders and weekly performance analytics.

  1. Task

Design the UX/UI of a mobile coaching app for professional athletes.

Business goals: cut the time it takes to start a workout and increase conversion from the free trial to a paid full-price subscription.

The client’s wish: a premium glass effect in the interface.

  1. Research

To understand why athletes don’t reach payment and take long to start a workout, I studied the market, the funnel and the conditions in which the app is used.

Competitor audit. What works for them and what users complain about. Takeaway: take the best, avoid their mistakes and stand out with features and design rather than price.

Conversion funnel. In fitness apps the paywall appears either right at launch or after a dull questionnaire (height, weight, goal). People pay without understanding what for. Takeaway: for an athlete to buy a full-price subscription, they need to feel the “endowment effect” before paying.

Context of use. In the gym the athlete has sweaty hands, shakes after a set, is out of breath, and the lighting causes glare. There’s no time to read small text. Takeaway: the interface must be large, high-contrast and controlled in a couple of taps.

The role of content. A professional already knows the basic technique. What brings them back is video instructions from top coaches with unique technique. Takeaway: video is the core of the product and the main argument for subscribing.

  1. Hypotheses

From the research takeaways I put together three ways to raise conversion to payment.

Hypothesis A — a discount on the first month. Lower the entry price. A quick effect, but it trains people to wait for discounts and doesn’t raise full-price payments — which is the main goal.

Hypothesis B — a shorter questionnaire, paywall in the same place. Fewer steps to payment. Less friction, but people still pay without feeling the product’s value.

Hypothesis C — value first, payment later. The athlete takes a free interactive test with video lessons and invests their own effort, and the paywall appears when the personal program is ready (the IKEA effect). Large video formats and a high-contrast interface make the app convenient right in the gym.

  1. Which hypothesis I chose and why

I went with hypothesis C — it’s the only one that answers all the research takeaways:

  • it creates the endowment effect before payment;

  • it raises full-price payments rather than relying on discounts;

  • it makes video the main value people pay for;

  • it accounts for gym conditions.

Hypothesis A contradicts the full-price goal, and hypothesis B doesn’t remove the main reason people drop off — not understanding what they pay for.

  1. Designing the mockups

  1. Paywall at the end of the test (UX funnel). I rebuilt the onboarding: first an initial physical test with a metronome and professional video lessons, then the system reports that the personal program is ready — and only then offers a subscription.

    Why it works: people pay not for a promise but for a program already calculated for their physiology and one they invested their own effort in.

  1. The video player at the center of the workout (UI). The active workout screen is built around a large video: just the exercise, a timer and basic buttons.

    Why it works: the product’s main value is in view at every workout, and without visual noise the athlete gets to the set faster.

  1. Large high-contrast elements (UX). Tap targets and indicators are designed for sweaty hands and post-set tremor.

    Why it works: you hit the right button on the first try — the workout starts faster, and the app is easy to use between sets, so people come back to it.

  1. Glassmorphism and liquid glass — functionally (UI). A deep black background, a neon-yellow accent, and glass only in cards, modals and alerts.

    Why it works: the premium look justifies a full-price subscription, while the glass doesn’t get in the way of the main thing — the video — and everything stays readable in any gym lighting.

  1. Presentation and defense

I walked the client through the whole chain: research takeaways, three hypotheses, the choice and the purpose of each decision.

The main thing to defend was the late paywall. The first payment comes later, which looks like a risk. The argument: the goal is full-price subscriptions, and people pay more willingly for what they’ve already invested in; an early paywall brings quick but rare payments.

Glass isn’t just for looks. The client’s wish is met, but the effect is used only where it doesn’t hurt the video or legibility in the gym. I backed the decisions with UX test results.

  1. Handoff to development

The design project is built systematically: all screens, element states and modals are organized, built with Auto Layout and linked to a UI kit with a design system, styles and components in all states. Animations are annotated. The mockups are handed off clean and predictable, which cuts development time several times over.

  1. Results

Full-price conversion +10%: moving payment to the end of the test sharply raised the motivation to buy — the athlete pays for a program already calculated for them.

Time-to-value −30%: thanks to video formats and minimalist navigation, users go straight to the workout.

Retention +20%: convenience in the gym and high-contrast alerts built a habit of using the service as a full-fledged coach at every workout.

  1. Where and how I used AI

  • Data analysis. I scraped reviews of competitor apps from the App Store and Google Play and ran them through AI for semantic analysis and clustering of UX pain points — key patterns in a couple of minutes.

  • Flow design. With an LLM I analyzed UX patterns in the fitness segment, modeled the user journey (CJM), built the funnel logic and justified when to show the paywall.

  • Content for testing mockups. I generated realistic workout footage with complex lighting to check how legible the interface is over video before any video production.

  • Testing. AI plugins in Figma for heatmaps and perception simulation — I calculated tap-target proportions without hours of manual contrast tests.

  • Figma routine. An AI agent handled layer naming, styles, design system descriptions and checking component states.
