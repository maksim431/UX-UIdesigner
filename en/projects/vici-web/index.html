  1. Project at a glance

  • Role. I was the only designer on the team.

  • Term. 2 months from the briefing to the transfer to the development of layouts with adaptations.

  • Starting point. A redesign after a rebrand. The old website didn’t help sell the products.

  • Outcome. A new website for all devices where recipes and products are linked.

  1. The task

The task was to make a new website in the brand’s updated style, so that people spend more time on it and then buy the products in a store. The website has no cart, so it has to sell through dish ideas.

Technical condition from the client: pages had to open fast on mobile internet right in the store, so heavy video and animation couldn’t be used.

  1. What I found out

Why people come. People don’t look for fish specs, they look for an idea of what to cook. So I decided to make the website a cooking magazine.

Where the dead ends were. On the old website products and recipes were separate, so people hit a dead end and left.

Where people visit from. Often from a phone right at the shelf. So everything has to be easy to use with one hand.

  1. Three hypotheses and how I chose

I scored the hypotheses with ICE. Impact: how much time on site and purchases at the shelf would change (1 to 10). Confidence: how sure I am of the result (in percent). Ease: how easy it is to build (1 to 10). The total is the product of the three scores.

Hypothesis A. A beautiful catalog. The site becomes more modern, but the dead ends stay.
Impact 3/10 · Confidence 60% · Ease 5/10 → ICE 9

Hypothesis B. A separate recipe blog. The recipes will inspire people but won’t lead them to a product.
Impact 5/10 · Confidence 50% · Ease 5/10 → ICE 12.5

Hypothesis C. A magazine where recipes and products are linked. People pick a dish and immediately see what to buy.
Impact 9/10 · Confidence 70% · Ease 3/10 → ICE 18.9

I chose C because it has the highest ICE score: 18.9 versus 9 and 12.5. It is the only one that both keeps people on the website and leads them to the shelf.

  1. Where I was wrong and what I changed

A recipe that led nowhere. In the first version I kept the old structure, where products and recipes were separate. Then I went through the shopper’s path on the prototype: I’m looking for a dinner idea and want to know what to buy. The path broke off at the end of the recipe. So I linked recipes and products, and this became the main decision of the project.

A blue that was hard to read. The brand blue on a white background was too pale for text. I noticed this when I checked contrast. For text I adjusted the shade, and in large accents I kept the original color.

  1. What I did and why

  1. Products and recipes are linked. A product card has a “What to cook with it” block, and a recipe has an “Ingredients from VICI” block.

    Why: people leave the website with a dinner plan and know which pack to look for.

  1. Similar recipes. After a recipe the website suggests the next one.

    Why: it is always clear where to go next.

  1. Tags “Quick”, “For breakfast”, “Seafood”. In recipes and products.

    Why: people find what they need right away.

  1. A mobile version for the shopper at the shelf. Large buttons and a recipe that is easy to scroll with one hand. To keep pages fast in the store, the look comes from photos and fonts, not video.

    Why: the website helps right at the shelf, like a cookbook on your phone.

  1. Defending the design and the developer handoff

The client’s main question was how to tell whether a website without a cart helps sales. I suggested looking at how many people go from recipes to products, how many pages they view and whether they come back from their phones.

I handed the developers tidy mockups and a ready map showing which recipe leads to which product.

How I work. When the prototype showed a dead end in my own first version, I rebuilt the structure and didn’t hold on to the convenient solution.

  1. Results

I don’t have numbers after launch yet, so I describe what I changed and what it should affect.

What became easier on the website

  • No dead ends: from a recipe you can go to a product and back. More people will get to the right pack.

  • The next recipe is always nearby, so people stay on the website longer.

  • Tags help people find what they need faster.

  • A light mobile version: easy and fast right in the store.

How the website tells the brand’s story

  • Shows dishes, not just products, like a cooking magazine.

  • The brand’s products are visible in every recipe.

  • A light modern style helps move away from the “cheap frozen food” image.

  1. How AI helped

  • Claude: data collection and analysis. Reviews, search queries and links between recipes and products. There are many more sources now, so the conclusions are more accurate. 5 minutes instead of a full day of manual searching.

  • Claude + Figma Agent: preparing the file for developers. Styles, layer names, versions for different screens, the design system, checking for missing screens and the user path logic. 1 hour instead of 16 hours of work.

AI saves me time, and I make the decisions myself.
