sCPlab

SCP LAB is a trainer app for professional athletes. It picks a training program, shows video lessons from professionals and tracks progress.

in this case

  1. We stopped asking for money right away so that people buy the subscription at full price.

    First people try the app and get their own program, and then they pay.

  2. Buttons that are easy to hit in the middle of a set.

    Large buttons and full-screen video, everything works with one hand.

  3. Why I didn’t go with a discount, even though it is the easiest way to raise sales.

    Three hypotheses and how I chose between them.

  4. I tested the screen in a gym before the videos were shot.

    I made a picture with AI, took it to a gym and found a mistake before the shoot.

  1. Project at a glance

  • Role. I was the only designer on the team and did all the app design.

  • Term. 1 month from the briefing to submission for development.

  • Starting point. The app was built from scratch. All I had at the start was a screen map.

  • Outcome. 68 screens and a ready design system the developers use to build the app.

  1. The task

The client needed more people to buy a full-price subscription after the free period, and a workout had to be quick to start. The client also wanted a premium “glass” style.

Technical condition from the client: the subscription is sold only through the App Store. The payment window there is standard, and nothing in it can be changed or explained in our own words. So what people are paying for had to be explained earlier.

  1. What I found out

How people pay. Usually an app asks for money right away or after a long questionnaire, and people leave without understanding what to pay for. I decided that the value has to be shown before payment.

Where people use it. In the gym people have sweaty hands, are out of breath and see glare on the screen. So the buttons have to be large and there should be little text.

Why people come back. A professional already knows the basics. They need video lessons from top coaches, so video became the main part of the product.

  1. Three hypotheses and how I chose

I scored the hypotheses with ICE. Impact: how much full-price payments and retention would change (1 to 10). Confidence: how sure I am of the result (in percent). Ease: how easy it is to build (1 to 10). The total is the product of the three scores.

Hypothesis A. A first-month discount. It is quick, but people get used to waiting for discounts, and the client needs full-price payments.
Impact 3/10 · Confidence 60% · Ease 9/10 → ICE 16.2

Hypothesis B. A shorter questionnaire, payment in the same place. It gets simpler, but people still pay without trying anything.
Impact 4/10 · Confidence 50% · Ease 6/10 → ICE 12

Hypothesis C. Try first, pay later. A free test with video lessons, and the subscription appears when the personal program is ready.
Impact 9/10 · Confidence 70% · Ease 3/10 → ICE 18.9

I chose C because it has the highest ICE score: 18.9 versus 16.2 and 12. People pay more willingly for what they have already tried and put effort into. The downside is that the money comes a bit later. I told the client about it right away and suggested how to test the decision after launch.

  1. Where I was wrong and what I changed

The “play” icon. At first the steps on the main screen looked like ordinary cards. I saw that people paused at every step and didn’t immediately understand where to tap. I added a “play” icon, and it became clear that the card starts a video. There were fewer pauses.

Testing before the shoot. The videos hadn’t been shot yet, and I needed to know whether the screen is readable on top of video in a gym. I made a workout frame with bad lighting using AI, put the interface on top and went to an ordinary gym with my phone. I looked myself and asked visitors to read the screen.

There was a problem: the video progress icon on the right was too pale and got lost against the background. I made it higher-contrast, and now it is visible in any light. We found the mistake before the shoot and development, so fixing it cost the client nothing.

  1. What I did and why

  1. Payment at the end. First a free test with video lessons, then the message “your program is ready”, and only after that the subscription.

    How this solves the App Store condition: the standard payment window explains nothing, so we show our own screen before it. It says that the program is ready, what is in it and how much it costs. People reach the Apple window already knowing what they are paying for.

    Why: people pay for a program that is already built for them.

  1. Video at the center of the workout screen. A large player, a timer and a couple of buttons.

    Why: the main thing is always visible, and the workout starts faster.

  1. Large high-contrast buttons. I designed them for sweaty hands and heavy breathing after a set.

    Why: they are easy to hit in the middle of a workout, and the app is easy to use right in the gym.

  1. Style. Black background, neon yellow accent, “glass” only in cards and windows.

    Why: the app looks expensive, so paying full price for the subscription feels fine. And everything reads in any light.

  1. Defending the design and the developer handoff

I showed the client everything in order: what I found, what the hypotheses were and why I made each decision. The late payment raised the most questions, because the money comes a bit later. I explained that people pay full price in return. We agreed to keep the “glass” only where it doesn’t get in the way of video.

I handed the developers a tidy file: all screens, button states and animations with comments. That way development goes faster and without extra questions, and it costs the client less.

How I work. For eight months I was the only designer, so I explained every decision through the business goal and talked openly about risks.

  1. Results

The app is only now launching, so I describe what I changed and what it should affect.

What became easier in the app itself

  • A new entry into the app: first the test and your own program, then payment. This should bring more full-price subscriptions.

  • A simple workout screen: video, timer and a couple of buttons within one hand’s reach. The workout starts faster.

  • Clear where to tap: the “play” icon on the steps, fewer pauses on the way to the first workout.

  • A visible video indicator: you see at once how much is left.

  • A reason to come back: smart reminders and a weekly progress report.

How the app explains its value

  • Value first, then price: coach videos and your own program are visible before payment.

  • Clear what you pay for: after the message “your program is ready”, payment looks like the clear next step.

  • Looks like a tool for professionals: the premium style supports the full price.

  • Everything reads quickly: large text and contrast help even in the gym between sets.

  1. How AI helped

  • Claude: data collection and analysis. Competitor reviews and the user path to payment. There are many more sources now, so the conclusions are more accurate. 5 minutes instead of a full day of manual searching.

  • Gemini: workout frames. With them I tested the screen on top of video in a gym before the shoot.

  • Claude + Figma Agent: preparing the file for developers. Styles, layer names, versions for different screens, the design system, checking for missing screens and the user path logic. 1 hour instead of 16 hours of work.

AI saves me time, and I make the decisions myself.
